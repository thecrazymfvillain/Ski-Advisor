These ranges are typical of most brands' color systems. Ranges vary between products, so the temperature printed on your wax wins.

Use snow temperature, not air temperature

Snow can't be warmer than 32°F (0°C). On clear nights and mornings, the snow surface is often a few degrees colder than the air, because it radiates heat away to the sky. A snow thermometer is the best way to know. Our resort pages estimate snow temperature from the forecast at mid-mountain.

Three things that change the pick

  • New snow: fresh crystals are sharp and cut into soft wax. Go one color colder than the temperature alone suggests.
  • Man-made snow: dense and abrasive. Also go one color colder, for durability.
  • Wet snow near freezing: use warm wax and brush extra structure into the base. See waxing for spring snow.

When two waxes overlap

Pick the colder one unless the snow is wet. Harder wax lasts longer, and if the forecast is off, a slightly cold wax costs less speed than a slightly warm one.

Universal wax

If you wax a few times a season, an all-temperature universal wax is a sensible choice. It won't be the fastest on any given day, but it's never badly wrong.

Fluoro-free

Fluorinated waxes are banned in FIS racing, and major brands have moved their lines to fluoro-free formulas. Everything we recommend is fluoro-free.

Reading the forecast for wax

Use the temperature where you'll ski, not in town. Temperatures usually drop as you go up, so the base area can be several degrees warmer than the summit. Our resort pages use each mountain's mid-mountain forecast for this reason. If the morning and afternoon fall in different ranges, wax for the hours you'll actually ski, or for the colder end if you'll ski all day.

Brand colors don't match

Most brands use colors for temperature, but the colors don't line up between brands. One brand's red can cover a different range from another's, and some lines skip colors entirely. Always read the temperature range printed on the package. The colors in our chart follow a common system so you can compare ranges, not a rule for every brand.

Wax tiers

  • Universal or all-temperature: covers a wide range. Never perfect, never badly wrong.
  • Performance or hydrocarbon: made for a temperature range, faster than universal when it matches the snow, and good value for regular skiing and training.
  • Race: harder-wearing, faster blends for a narrower range, at several times the price. Mostly worth it for racing; see race wax vs training wax.

Graphite and other additives

Graphite and similar additives help on dirty spring snow and in very dry, cold conditions, where static and dirt slow a base down. They're optional for most recreational skiing.

Quick °F and °C conversions

32°F is 0°C, 23°F is −5°C, 14°F is −10°C, 5°F is −15°C and −4°F is −20°C. Each 10°C step is 18°F.
