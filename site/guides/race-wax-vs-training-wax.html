Race Wax vs Training Wax

Race wax is built for speed on one day. Training wax is built to survive a week of hard laps. Here's how racers use both.

Two different jobs

Training days are about durability. Race courses are usually hard, injected or man-made snow that strips wax quickly, and you're doing many laps. Race day is about speed for a few runs. That's why racers use cheaper, tougher wax for training and save the expensive wax for races.

Training wax

  • A fluoro-free performance wax matched to the temperature. One layer is enough.
  • Re-wax every few training days. Hard training snow wears it off fast.
  • When you're between two waxes, pick the colder, harder one.

Race-day wax, in three layers

  1. Base layer: a hard, cold wax ironed in, cooled and scraped. It helps the race wax last on abrasive snow. Skip it on soft, warm days.
  2. Race wax: a fluoro-free race wax matched to the snow temperature, ironed in, cooled fully, scraped and brushed. Many racers build two or three layers over the week before a race.
  3. Top coat, race morning: a fluoro-free race top coat (block, powder or liquid) in the same temperature range, applied per its instructions and corked or brushed in.

The fluoro ban

Fluorinated waxes are banned in FIS racing, and races test skis for them. If your skis ever had fluoro wax, clean the base thoroughly with several hot-scrapes before racing, and check your club or league's rules.

Edges on race day

Give the side edge a final polish with a fine stone the night before. Most racers run a 3° side edge with 0.5° to 0.7° base. See edge angles explained.

Our resort pages have a Training day / Race day switch for racers that changes the wax plan and the tuning steps.

A race-week waxing schedule

  1. Early in the week: clean the base with a hot-scrape and iron in a hard base layer. Train on a durable performance wax.
  2. Mid-week: re-wax training skis after hard sessions; abrasive training snow strips wax quickly.
  3. Two nights before: iron in the first race-wax layer and let it cool overnight.
  4. The night before: a second race-wax layer, scraped and brushed, then a final edge polish with a fine stone.
  5. Race morning: check the snow temperature, apply the top coat if you use one, and brush.

Does the event matter?

Glide matters most where skis run flat and fast: speed events and the flatter sections of GS courses. In slalom, edge grip and line matter more than a few hundredths of glide, so many racers put more care into edges than into expensive top coats for slalom.

Cleaning old fluoro wax off your bases

If skis were ever waxed with fluorinated wax, clean them before racing under fluoro-free rules. Several hot-scrapes with a soft cleaning wax lift it out of the base; scrape while warm each time. Use separate brushes for fluoro-free wax, because old brushes can carry fluorinated residue back onto a clean base. Check your league's rules and testing procedures.

Spending where it counts

Race wax and top coats cost several times as much as training wax. Most junior and masters racers get the best value from a good base layer and race wax, and save top coats for important races.
