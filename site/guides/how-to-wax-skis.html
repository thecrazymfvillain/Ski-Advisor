How to Wax Skis at Home

A full hot wax takes about an hour of work plus cooling time. Here's the whole process, and the mistakes that undo it.

What you need

  • A waxing iron. A dedicated ski iron holds a steady temperature. Clothes irons run hot and uneven, and their steam holes leave stripes.
  • Wax for the temperature you'll ski. See the wax temperature chart, or use a universal wax if you only wax a few times a season.
  • A plastic (plexi) scraper and a nylon brush. Brass and horsehair brushes are worth adding later.
  • Something to hold the ski: a vise, or straps on a sturdy table. A thick rubber band holds the brakes up.

Step by step

  1. Secure the ski base-up. Loop a rubber band over the brake arms so they stay out of the way.
  2. Clean the base. Brush tip to tail. If the base is dirty, hot-scrape it: iron on a soft, warm wax and scrape it off while it's still warm. It pulls dirt out of the base.
  3. Set the iron. Use the temperature on the wax label. Most waxes melt between about 250°F and 300°F (120–150°C). Colder, harder waxes need more heat.
  4. Drip the wax. Hold the bar against the iron and let it drip in a line down each side of the base.
  5. Iron it in. Spread the wax in slow, steady passes from tip to tail. Keep the iron moving. The topsheet should feel warm to the touch, never hot.
  6. Let it cool. At least 30 minutes at room temperature, overnight if you can. Wax keeps soaking in and hardening as it cools.
  7. Scrape. Pull the plexi scraper tip to tail until only fine dust comes off. Clear the edges and sidewalls with the scraper's notch.
  8. Brush. Brush tip to tail until the base shines. For cold, hard wax, start with brass, then nylon. For warm wax, nylon then horsehair.

Mistakes that ruin a wax job

  • An iron that smokes the wax. If you see smoke, the iron is too hot and can damage the base.
  • Stopping the iron in one spot. It can burn or blister the base. Keep moving.
  • Scraping while the wax is warm. You pull the wax back out of the base.
  • Leaving too much on. Unscraped wax makes skis slow and grabby. Scrape until you're sure you're done, then scrape again.

How long it takes

Plan on about an hour of hands-on work for a pair: ten minutes to set up and clean, fifteen to iron in the wax, then fifteen to scrape and twenty to brush once the wax has cooled. The cooling time in between is what most people cut short. Wax ironed in the evening and scraped the next morning is harder and lasts longer than wax scraped after twenty minutes.

Hot wax vs rub-on and liquid wax

A hot wax melts into the base and lasts the longest, usually several ski days. Rub-on, paste and liquid waxes sit mostly on the surface. They're quick and fine for a touch-up at the car or in the lodge, but they wear off within a day or two. Use them between hot waxes, not instead of them.

Getting the iron temperature right

Start at the temperature on the wax label. If the iron drags or the wax won't spread in a thin, wet-looking film, turn it up a little. If the wax smokes or the topsheet gets hot to the touch, turn it down. Harder, colder waxes need more heat than soft warm ones, so if you switch wax colors, reset the iron too. Never leave a hot iron resting on the base.

Getting a better result

  • Thin and even beats thick. Drip just enough wax to cover the base in one slow pass. Excess wax only means more scraping.
  • Two layers for cold or abrasive snow. Iron, cool, scrape and brush a first coat, then repeat. The second coat goes in deeper.
  • Brush longer than you think. The base should look clean and slightly glossy, with the structure lines visible again.

Waxing indoors

Lay cardboard or an old sheet under the bench; wax shavings get everywhere and are slippery on hard floors. Keep the room ventilated, and keep the iron moving: fumes come from overheated wax, so if you see smoke, the iron is too hot. Unplug the iron as soon as you're done.

When to take your skis to a shop

Home waxing doesn't replace a shop tune. Take your skis in for deep gouges that show the core, a base that's no longer flat, edges with serious damage, or a fresh stone grind, which resets the base structure and the base bevel. Many skiers get a shop tune once a season and wax at home in between.
